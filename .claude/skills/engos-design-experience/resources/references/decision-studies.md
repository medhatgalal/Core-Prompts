# Compose the decision, then expose the right detail

Load when structure, information priority, visual direction, or density could change the outcome. Use only the part that resolves the current uncertainty.

## Give the working view a clear job

Start with what the person is trying to see or do now. Establish a focal relationship, supporting comparisons, and the next meaningful action. Use realistic content density early: long names, a genuine exception, a selected or changed item, and relevant controls. Inspect the composition at its actual size before spreading it across the experience.

Preserve the strengths of an owned foundation: recognizable vocabulary, type hierarchy, useful density, established actions, and native conventions. When direction is open, inspect a relevant visual reference or worked example and translate what serves this task: reading rhythm, alignment, contrast, grouping, imagery role, or interaction. The [diagnostic before/after](../examples/worked-decisions.md) is useful for continuity versus density; it is not a universal layout or an unqualified success.

Art direction should be visible in composition, not only adjectives. A distinctive identity may help; familiar patterns may fit better. Neither novelty nor library conformity establishes quality.

Different experiences need different compositions. In an editorial field journal, a wide image may establish place while a strong opening line sets the question; text measure, image scale, captions, and section rhythm guide reading. Inspect whether the sequence gives evidence the right weight and supports both reading and return. A ledger of metrics may contribute little. In a native audio editor, the timeline or waveform may deserve most of the space; nearby controls serve frequent edits while an inspector reveals selected-clip detail. Keep selection and playback meaning stable as the inspector changes. Check input, focus, and interruption in the native runtime. These are conditional examples, not required shells.

## Allocate attention by consequence

These are choices about information, not required interface sections:

| Information | Treatment to consider | Failure to watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Current object, saved/draft basis, status | Compact, unambiguous context near the current task or restored on return. | A persistent summary consumes the space needed to compare or act. |
| Difference that changes the decision | Show the changed value and practical consequence, with units and relevant condition. | Names and totals omit the qualitative exchange; a paraphrase drops an important qualification. |
| Full description or secondary evidence | One clear home with direct access and a useful return; reveal in place when comparison needs it. | Details are technically present but hard to find, compare, or return from. |
| Unchanged material | Keep recognizable; expand when it contributes to the decision. | Repeating full text makes differences harder to detect. |

“Available on demand” is not permission to hide a constraint, uncertainty, or requirement that changes the choice. Keep the short qualifier with the measure; reveal the supporting explanation separately when useful. Likewise, showing everything initially can be right for unfamiliar or consequential choices. Choose from the task, not a disclosure rule.

Repeating a short identity, critical condition, or local action status can improve orientation. Repeating complete source prose is useful when the new context needs it, but carries a cost when it adds no decision value. The criterion is whether repetition helps this use, not whether the words appeared before.

Before shrinking text or creating another panel, look for repeated material, weak grouping, or unnecessary hierarchy levels. Spend recovered space where it helps: readable comparison columns, more neighboring alternatives, a clear next action, or breathing room around decisive evidence. Compactness means useful information at a usable size, not maximum occupancy.

## A small authored transformation

Suppose a fictional fieldwork draft replaces a covered site with an exposed one. A name-only readback hides the practical exchange; repeating both full site descriptions obscures the change.

An intermediate treatment might read: **“Replace Cedar shelter with North bank: longer recording range; weather exposed. Adds 20 minutes. Safe access depends on the footbridge check.”** Keep the original descriptions and access notes directly available. This text is illustrative, not verified domain guidance.

The short readback works only if those are the actual decision-changing differences. If access depends on several interacting conditions, show their full comparison. If the user is merely correcting a typo, no new readback is needed. Content compression should preserve meaning, not manufacture a recommendation.

## Compare the consequential alternative

Include the active draft or existing product. Make a sketch, rendered mockup, or connected slice at enough fidelity to reveal the uncertainty. Use comparable content and attempt the same task through the options. A density choice needs actual text; a recovery choice needs states; a hierarchy choice needs a render. Do not build whole applications merely to compare one unsettled relationship.

Inspect benefit and cost together: context retained, facts displaced, alternatives visible, qualifier legibility, interaction burden, and action access. The change must earn what it occupies. Do not force all facts above a fold or infer simultaneous visibility from a full-page image.

Show requested alternatives before commitment unless selection is delegated. Preserve settled directions; the number and form of options follow the decision. Stop exploring when the next consequential choice is sufficiently supported.
